New Mexico Water Research Brief · 27 November 2026

Statewide relief, persistent operational divergence.

New Mexico’s drought classification improved sharply, but reservoir, river-reach, snow and legal-operational conditions still tell different stories. Decision support must preserve those distinctions.

Published 27 November 2026 · 08:51 MST · Public-safe edition

Read First

Read First · 1

U.S. Drought Monitor — New Mexico, 24 November

NDMC, NOAA and USDA · Released 25 November 2026 · State map

Evidence quality: High for weekly drought classification; it is a multi-indicator expert synthesis, not a direct water-availability measurement.

What is new: Severe, extreme, and exceptional drought fell to zero statewide. About 10.24% remained in moderate drought and 33.29% abnormally dry, while 56.48% was drought-free.

Practical implication: Recognize the significant short-term improvement, but retain separate groundwater, storage, snowpack, compact, demand, and deliverability indicators.

Full reading: Yes—the one-page map and comparison table are the clearest statewide weekly signal.

Read First · 2

Navajo Reservoir 40-Day Operations Data

Bureau of Reclamation · Data through 26 November 2026 · Operations table

Evidence quality: High-authority provisional federal operations data, subject to later review.

What is new: Storage increased from roughly 1.479 million acre-feet on 20 November to 1.491 million on 26 November. On 26 November, provisional inflow was 1,467 cfs against a 650 cfs release.

Practical implication: Update near-term storage and release scenarios while tracking how quickly inflows recede. Distinguish total reservoir storage from water legally or contractually available to a specific New Mexico user.

Full reading: Yes for San Juan Basin, operations, compact, and supply-planning staff.

Read First · 3

Rio Grande Flow and Reservoir Conditions — 24 November

International Boundary and Water Commission · 24 November 2026 · Daily report

Evidence quality: High-authority provisional binational operations data.

What is new: Downstream flows remained extremely low: approximately 0.03 cubic metres per second below Caballo Dam and 0.20 at El Paso in the reported morning observations.

Practical implication: Statewide drought improvement and northern inflow gains do not imply uniform Rio Grande availability. Use reach-specific flows, releases, project accounting, and delivery obligations.

Full reading: Yes for lower-Rio Grande and compact operations teams.

Additional Priority Reading

Caballo Reservoir Status — 24 November

New Mexico Water data service using public observations · 24 November 2026 · Facility dashboard

Evidence quality: High for compiled observations; operating and legal decisions should use agency records.

What is new: Caballo held about 26,464 acre-feet—8.2% of full pool and near the historical median for the date—despite the dramatic moisture and storage gains elsewhere.

Practical implication: Evaluate recovery facility by facility. Percent-full comparisons should be paired with purpose, release rules, downstream demand, sediment-adjusted capacity, and ownership.

Full reading: Targeted reading for reservoir and lower-basin staff.

Early-Season Snow Conditions: Rio Grande–Albuquerque Watershed

NOAA SNODAS, USDA NRCS and USGS data synthesis · Data through 21 November 2026 · Snow dashboard

Evidence quality: Moderate to high for early-season situational awareness; modeled snow estimates remain uncertain and the watershed has no listed local snow station on this page.

What is new: The dashboard establishes the opening 2026–27 snow-water-equivalent trajectory against the 2004–2025 distribution.

Practical implication: Treat the early curve as a baseline, not a forecast. Monitor basin SWE, elevation distribution, temperature, storm sequencing, and runoff efficiency before changing spring assumptions.

Full reading: Yes for planning staff; revisit as observations accumulate.

Executive Implications

  • New Mexico’s weekly drought signal improved markedly. That is meaningful short-term relief, not proof of aquifer or system-wide recovery.
  • Water conditions remain spatially asymmetric. Navajo storage continues rising while lower-Rio Grande flow and Caballo storage remain constrained.
  • Keep planning ledgers separate. Climate classification, physical storage, legal ownership, compact accounting, delivery capacity, and demand answer different questions.

Bottom line: The emerging pattern is divergence after relief: statewide drought categories are improving faster than some downstream operational and structural water constraints.
