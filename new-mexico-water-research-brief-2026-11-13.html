New Mexico Water Research Brief · 13 November 2026

Fall storms changed storage faster than long-term risk.

Drought classifications improved again and major reservoirs captured extraordinary inflows. The decisive question is how much of that gain persists through winter, operations and interstate accounting.

U.S. Drought Monitor — 10 November Conditions

U.S. Drought Monitor / NOAA / USDA / NASA partners · Released 12 November 2026 · Official summary

Evidence quality: High for weekly broad-scale drought classification; it integrates indicators and expert review but is not supply or compact accounting.

What is new: Additional improvements reached New Mexico despite a relatively dry week because recent rainfall produced near-record soil wetness and rapidly filling reservoirs. Drought.gov estimates about 626,900 New Mexico residents remain in drought areas, down 16.2% from the prior week.

Practical implication: Update public posture and short-term triggers while preserving separate long-term groundwater, reservoir and compact indicators.

Full read? Essential for statewide communication and drought posture.

Navajo Reservoir 40-Day Operations Record

Bureau of Reclamation · Updated 12 November 2026 · Reclamation dataset

Evidence quality: High primary operational data; values are provisional and subject to review.

What is new: Storage rose from 841,808 acre-feet on 12 October to 1,436,157 acre-feet on 11 November—a gain of 594,349 acre-feet, roughly 71%. The Drought Monitor reports the reservoir is now above its average storage for this time of year.

Practical implication: Rebuild San Juan scenarios using updated storage, inflow, release, demand and operating constraints. Do not apply this northern-basin gain to Rio Grande conditions.

Full read? Essential for San Juan Basin and regional supply planning.

Elephant Butte Reservoir Operations Record

Bureau of Reclamation · Updated 12 November 2026 · Reclamation dataset

Evidence quality: High primary operational data; provisional and subject to revision.

What is new: Reported storage increased from 166,586 acre-feet on 5 November to 208,084 acre-feet on 11 November—another 41,498 acre-feet, about 25%, after the prior week’s sharp rise.

Practical implication: Treat the gain as improved operating flexibility, not supply normalization. Reconcile it with conservation capacity, evaporation, Rio Grande Project demand, sediment-adjusted storage and compact accounting.

Full read? Essential for Rio Grande Project and executive supply decisions.

ENSO Diagnostic Discussion — 12 November

NOAA Climate Prediction Center · 12 November 2026 · NOAA discussion

Evidence quality: High for official probabilistic climate assessment; it does not predict basin-specific precipitation or runoff.

What is new: NOAA places the probability of an historically strong El Niño—exceeding prior events dating to 1950—near 60% for October–December and November–January.

Practical implication: Expand wet-winter, flood, sediment, snowpack and runoff scenarios while retaining dry-side contingencies. Tie decisions to observed SWE, temperature, soil state and runoff efficiency.

Full read? Recommended for seasonal planning; not a standalone operating basis.

New Mexico Climate and Drought Indicators

NOAA/NIDIS Drought.gov · Updated 12 November 2026 · State dashboard

Evidence quality: High for source-attributed, multi-indicator monitoring; layers update on different schedules.

What is new: October 2026 ranked as New Mexico’s 20th-wettest October since 1895, with 2.15 inches of statewide precipitation—1.00 inch above normal. January–October ranked 35th wettest.

Practical implication: Preserve the distinction between statewide climate anomaly and basin response. Track how precipitation becomes soil moisture, streamflow, reservoir storage and groundwater recharge.

Full read? Recommended for executive context and decision-support design.

Executive implications

  • Reservoir gains are material and spatially uneven. Navajo and Elephant Butte must remain separate operating stories.
  • Drought relief is broader than last week. Long-term groundwater, storage and legal constraints still need their own triggers.
  • A historic El Niño remains possible, not guaranteed. Scenario breadth matters more than a single forecast narrative.

Bottom line: New Mexico should update storage and drought baselines now, then measure retention through winter rather than assuming recent inflows permanently changed dependable supply.
